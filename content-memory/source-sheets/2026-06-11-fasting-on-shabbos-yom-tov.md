# Fasting On Shabbos And Yom Tov

## Research Status / Non-Psak Note

This note is source research only. It maps the main Sefaria-verified sources on
fasting on Shabbos and Yom Tov and summarizes the apparent issues for
Shloimie/rav review. It is not an automated final psak.

All cited sources below were checked against Sefaria source pages or the
Sefaria v3 text API on 2026-06-11. Sefaria search was useful but uneven for
this topic: English search queries found relevant sugyos such as
[Eruvin 40b:18](https://www.sefaria.org/Eruvin.40b.18), while exact Hebrew
searches for `תענית חלום שבת` returned no hits and some queries produced
partial shard errors. Therefore the final source map relies on directly
verified Sefaria text pages.

## Working Source Synthesis

The sources strongly frame Shabbos as a day of eating and oneg, and Yom Tov as
a day with simcha and a required human-facing `lachem` component. Ordinary
fasting is therefore treated as conflicting with the day. The halacha codes
prohibit fasting on Shabbos even until the sixth hour, and Shulchan Aruch
Orach Chayim 529 explicitly points Yom Tov fasting rules back to the Shabbos
rules in Orach Chayim 288.

The major exceptions and edge cases are not simple. Yom Kippur is an explicit
Torah fast even when it coincides with Shabbos. A taanit chalom has a Gemara
basis even on Shabbos, but Shulchan Aruch and Mishnah Berurah sharply qualify
when that applies and require a compensating weekday fast for the lost Shabbos
oneg. If eating is physically or emotionally harmful, the codes recognize that
not eating may itself be oneg in some cases. Public fasts that fall on Shabbos
are postponed, while Taanit Esther is advanced when needed.

## Halachic Detail Buckets

### Ordinary Shabbos Fasting

Shabbos fasting is broadly prohibited because it contradicts oneg Shabbos.
Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 288:1 prohibits fasting until the sixth hour, and
Mishnah Berurah 288:1 treats a shorter period as forbidden when it is done as a
fast. The Gemara and Rambam frame oneg through food and drink according to a
person's means, not necessarily lavish eating.

### When Eating Causes Pain Or Harm

Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 288:2 says that if eating is harmful, then not
eating can be oneg. Mishnah Berurah 288:3 extends the practical discussion to a
case where even a kezayit would be hard. This needs case-specific rav/medical
review because the sources do not turn ordinary dieting, preference, or
unverified discomfort into a blanket license to fast.

### Taanit Chalom On Shabbos

Taanit 12b gives a basis for fasting a dream fast even on Shabbos, and
Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 288:4 codifies that possibility. But the same
sources require a later fast for having fasted on Shabbos. Shulchan Aruch
288:5 and Mishnah Berurah 288:7 and 288:15 significantly narrow the practical
case: not all dreams qualify, today there is concern that we are not expert in
dream interpretation, and fasting is only considered when it is itself a relief
to the distressed person.

### Erev Shabbos Fasts

Mishnah Taanit 4:3 and Taanit 26a show concern for entering Shabbos with kavod.
Eruvin 40b discusses whether an Erev Shabbos fast is completed. Shulchan Aruch
Orach Chayim 249:4 gives the code-level structure: accepted Friday fasts,
personal fasts, public fasts, and dream fasts may have different completion
rules.

### Yom Tov

Pesachim 68b, Beitzah 15b, Rambam Hilchot Yom Tov 6:17-18, and Shulchan Aruch
Orach Chayim 529 frame Yom Tov as requiring simcha, eating/drinking, and care
for others. Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 529:2 explicitly sends the law of
fasting on Yom Tov to the Shabbos fasting siman, Orach Chayim 288. Rosh
Hashanah has its own note in Orach Chayim 597:1: eat, drink, and rejoice, but
with restraint so the awe of the day remains.

### Public Fasts And Yom Kippur

Rambam Hilchot Taaniyot 5:5 and Shulchan Aruch Orach Chayim 550:3 say the four
public fasts are postponed when they fall on Shabbos. Shulchan Aruch Orach
Chayim 686:2 advances Taanit Esther when Purim would be Sunday. Yom Kippur is
different: Leviticus 23:27 and 23:32,
[Mishnah Yoma 8:1](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishnah_Yoma.8.1), and Shulchan
Aruch Orach Chayim 611:1 define it as a Torah fast and affliction day.

## Open Points For Shloimie / Rav Review

1. What exact case is being asked: voluntary fast, public fast, personal neder,
   taanit chalom, medical inability to eat, emotional distress, Erev Shabbos
   fast, Yom Tov, Rosh Hashanah, or Yom Kippur?
2. If the claim is that eating harms the person, does that mean actual medical
   harm, significant pain, psychological distress, or ordinary discomfort?
3. If the case is a dream, does it meet the narrow taanit chalom criteria, and
   is a Shabbos fast appropriate today versus weekday fasting, tzedakah,
   hatavat chalom, or another response?
4. How should weak, ill, pregnant, nursing, elderly, or minor individuals be
   treated in this case?
5. If someone accepted a personal fast or has a neder, what exactly was
   accepted and did the person specify completion, especially around Erev
   Shabbos?
6. Which minhag/community should govern Yom Tov or Rosh Hashanah edge cases?

## Working Conclusion

The source pattern is consistent: Shabbos and Yom Tov are generally not days
for fasting, because Shabbos requires oneg and Yom Tov requires simcha and a
`lachem` component. Yom Kippur is the explicit Torah exception. Other exceptions
exist, especially taanit chalom and cases where eating is itself harmful, but
they are narrow and should be reviewed with a rav before applying them in
practice.
